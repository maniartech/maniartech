Choosing a software development partner is a trust decision made with almost no trustworthy information. Every agency website says senior team, proven process, delighted clients. The sites of good and bad vendors are nearly indistinguishable, because words are free.

So this post takes a different approach: instead of telling you what to look for in a partner, it teaches you how to audit one - any one. The test of the checklist is that it should be usable against us. At the end we run it against ourselves, briefly, with links; everything before that is vendor-neutral.

## How to read a case study

Case studies are the main evidence vendors offer, and most are written to resist exactly the scrutiny you should apply. Read them like this:

- **Look for a situation, not a logo.** A real case study describes a specific starting condition ("a lab running 600+ Access forms", "presales run on spreadsheets"), what was built, and what changed. If you cannot tell what problem existed before the vendor arrived, you are reading marketing, not evidence.
- **Named clients, or explained anonymity.** Naming a client is accountability - the claim can be checked. Anonymity is sometimes legitimate (confidentiality, a client not yet public), but a credible vendor tells you *why* a study is anonymized. A portfolio that is 100% unnamed and unexplained is a portfolio you cannot verify.
- **Hedged claims are a good sign.** Counterintuitive but reliable: a vendor who writes "by the client's account, still in use" or "launched recently - no outcomes to report yet" is showing you their calibration. Vendors who only ever report triumphs are showing you their copywriter.
- **Check the dates and the spread.** One glorious project from 2015 is different from work spanning years into the present. You want evidence the capability is alive now, not embalmed.
- **Ask what happened after go-live.** Longevity is the hardest thing to fake. "Is it still running? Who maintains it?" is a two-line email that eliminates a surprising number of vendors.

## What verifiable proof looks like

The dividing line in vendor evaluation is between claims you must take on faith and claims you can check from your chair. Verifiable proof includes:

- **Live systems you can visit.** A URL that works today beats any screenshot.
- **Credits on the client's own property.** A client publicly crediting the vendor on their own website is the client spending reputation on the vendor - far stronger than the vendor citing the client.
- **Public code and published work.** Open-source repositories, published specifications, technical writing. Not every good vendor publishes, but published work lets you judge quality directly instead of inferring it.
- **Certifications you can look up**, from bodies that actually audit - not badges the vendor awarded itself or bought from a listicle site.
- **References you choose.** Any vendor can produce two happy references. Asking "may I speak to a client from a project *like mine*" - and watching how they respond - tells you more than the call itself.

A practical rule: for every major claim on a vendor's site, ask "could I verify this in one click or one email?" Count the claims that fail. That count is your risk estimate.

## Seniority questions to ask

The most common failure mode in outsourced software is the bait-and-switch: impressive seniors in the sales calls, juniors on the keyboard. Questions that surface it:

- **"Who, by name, will write the code - and can I meet them before signing?"** Vague answers here predict vague answers everywhere.
- **"What is the ratio of engineers to salespeople and managers on my project?"** Layers of coordination around a junior core is the classic shape of the problem.
- **"Show me work by the actual individuals."** Not the company's portfolio - the assigned engineers' work: code, writing, systems they personally built.
- **"What happens if my assigned engineer leaves?"** You are listening for an answer about documentation and handover, not "we have a large bench" - a large bench is how you got juniors in the first place.
- **Have your most technical person talk to their most technical person.** Thirty minutes of real technical conversation is difficult to fake and routinely decisive.

## Ownership and lock-in questions

Lock-in rarely announces itself in the contract; it accumulates in the practicalities. Ask, in writing:

- **Who owns the code and IP on delivery** - and is that unambiguous in the contract?
- **Where do the repositories, cloud accounts, and domains live?** The correct answer is: in accounts you own, with the vendor as an invited collaborator. If the vendor owns the infrastructure, leaving them means migrating everything.
- **What stack is it built on, and could another team maintain it?** Mainstream, hireable technology is your exit guarantee. A proprietary framework only the vendor understands is lock-in even with perfect contracts - unless it is open, documented, and runnable without them, so weigh any vendor-owned platform by exactly that test.
- **What documentation ships with the system** - architecture, setup, runbooks - and can you see a sample from a past project?
- **"Walk me through what happens if we part ways in a year."** A good partner has a boring, specific answer. Hesitation is data.

## Process signals

You cannot inspect a vendor's process from outside, but you can probe its edges:

- **Certification or documentation.** ISO 9001 (quality) and ISO 27001 (security) certification means an external auditor has verified the process exists and is followed - not that the vendor is brilliant, but that they are systematic, and the certificates are checkable. Absent certification, ask to see the documented process; absent both, the process is whatever each project manager improvises.
- **How they estimate.** A vendor who quotes a firm price and date before understanding your workflow is guessing, and you will pay for the guess later. Look for stated assumptions, ranges, and phased commitments.
- **How they handle change and disagreement.** Ask "tell me about a time you told a client no." Partners who cannot produce an example will also not tell *you* no - and you are not paying for an echo.
- **First response quality.** The first substantive reply - who writes it, how specific it is, whether it asks real questions about your situation - is a free sample of the engagement.

## Red flags

Any one of these is a caution; several together are an answer:

- **Logo walls** - rows of famous logos with no story attached to any of them.
- **Vague superlatives as outcomes** - "delivered 500+ projects with 100% satisfaction" is a claim engineered to be uncheckable.
- **No named humans** - no founder, no engineers, no faces with accountability anywhere on the site.
- **Guaranteed price and timeline before discovery** - see above; the guess becomes your change-order bill.
- **Every technology, every industry, mastered** - a 40-item services menu from a 30-person shop means depth in none of it.
- **Prices only a junior team can produce** - senior engineering has a cost floor; a quote far below every other bid is telling you who will really do the work.
- **Pressure to sign fast** - discounts that expire Friday are for gym memberships, not multi-month engineering partnerships.
